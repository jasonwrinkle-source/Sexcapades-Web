Tests and imports

Sexcapades keeps each test loader’s input and preview tucked inside that profile, right inside the hypermap, so you can come back later without pasting everything all over again.

Not every item from KinkTest, Yes / No / Maybe, BDSMtest, or FetLife will land neatly on a topic. The preview puts any misses in italics, making them easy to review and rate by hand. For FetLife, choose a saved MHTML, MHT, HTML, HTM, or TXT page; Sexcapades reads only its Fetishes section and lets you know if a “View all” list looks truncated.

When the import finishes, the hypermap opens fully expanded with only the rated branches and concepts showing. You get a clean look at what landed before deciding what to fill in next.

Someone sent you a sync cardA type of social card, comparable to a business card. or QR Sheet—now what?

Here’s the clever part: the picture can also be the profile file. An original sync hypermap in SVG (recommended) or PNG carries the fullest backup; SVGZ packages the same sync hypermap more compactly, while a QR Sheet gives you black-on-white QR images, one code per image, for sharing in galleries and picture sets. Map PNGs no longer place an app-link QR over the artwork, so send the original file to preserve its embedded profile data; use a sync card or QR Sheet when you need visible, scannable codes. A profile share link is quicker still: open it and the profile name starts as Unknown; edit it when Sexcapades asks or accept it as-is, and the ratings arrive in a new profile tab.

To import from a QR Sheet: Open sexcapades.io → tap “Scan or Import” → upload the QR Sheet image or scan it with your camera. On mobile: the button goes straight to the camera scanner. On desktop: it opens the full import dialog with all options (file upload, paste, camera if available).

  1. Save the received image or file. Download the original export, sync-card image, or QR Sheet image from the text, chat, email, gallery, or picture set into your device’s files or photos when possible.
  2. Open Scan or Import. In Sexcapades, tap Scan or Import. On mobile, the button opens the camera scanner directly. On desktop, it opens the full import dialog with file upload, paste, and camera options when available.
  3. Choose the saved image or exported file. On desktop, use the full import dialog to upload it. Export options are SVG (recommended), SVGZ, PNG, and PDF. For round-trip import, use SVG (recommended), PNG, or PDF; SVGZ is export-only. For a JPEG or PNG QR Sheet image, it scans the visible QR code and restores the profile. A map PNG has no overlaid QR, so if a sharing service strips its embedded metadata, use the original file, a sync card, or a QR Sheet instead.
  4. Or scan the QR directly. On mobile, allow camera access when the scanner opens and point the camera at the profile-data QR. On desktop, choose the camera option in the full import dialog when one is available. For a large profile, scan the numbered QR Sheet images in any order; Sexcapades holds the pieces and reassembles the profile when the set is complete.
  5. Bring in your side. Create or import your own profile separately. When both are ready, select their tabs, then use Cast to set compatibility strictness and build the combined view without changing either person’s answers.

The QR path is made to survive real-world sharing. Profile codes use level-H error correction, a larger black-on-white rendering, and wider quiet zones, helping them stay readable after ordinary JPEG recompression—including after a chat app recompresses the photo.

On a true first run, Sexcapades opens in List View with the large Start — 54 topics, ~20 min button. List View: where the first run begins

List View gives you the map’s material in a clean outline. On first run, the large Start — 54 topics, ~20 min button begins the top-three-per-category pass. Each first-generation branch becomes a collapsible section, with its subtopics nested beneath it. Kinks are indented under their category — and under their subcategory, where one exists — with tree-view lines tracing the nesting, so the outline reads like the hypermap’s branches laid flat. Categories are ordered by synthesized popularity, most popular first, not alphabetically, and the kinks inside each category follow that same popularity order. Every topic keeps the same mind-map icon beside its name, so it’s easy to connect the outline to the visual map revealed after the first pass.

Each topic row has compact inline give and receive sliders, plus controls for ★ Favorite, × Hard Limit, and ⊘ Not Interested. Not Interested means “not for me, but not a hard boundary”; it stays distinct from a Hard Limit and from ☁ Fantasy only. The sliders behave exactly like their hypermap counterparts: drag past either end of the scale to clear a rating. On a directional topic, hover the row—or tap its tooltip trigger—to see the object being given and the object being received in the same popup style used on the hypermap. As you rate, the topic’s text name takes on its rating color; numbers, icons, and controls keep their default styling. Before you rate it, the name uses its hanky-code color; a topic without a hanky code keeps the default text color.

When you expand a first-generation category, topics that were already rated are hidden by default, leaving only what still needs your attention. Topics you rate while that category stays open remain in place; collapse and re-expand it, and those rated rows hide too. The list-view header has a Show prior ratings checkbox that decides what happens then: rated items stay visible while you work, but once their category or subcategory is collapsed and re-expanded, the checkbox state decides whether they show. Tick Show prior ratings to reveal rated topics—and keep them visible when categories are collapsed and reopened—then untick it to return to the unrated-only view.

Each category initially shows only its 3 most popular kinks. The Show the next three popular button, to the right of each category heading, reveals the next three with every click, and the ladder keeps climbing in multiples of three: 3 → 6 → 9 → 12 → 15 → 18 → 21 per category — 54, 108, 162, 216, 270, 324, then 378 topics across the map — until a category runs out and the full ~400 are on the table. There is no jump from 9 straight to everything; each category climbs at its own pace, one click of three more kinks at a time. Subcategories stay hidden unless the ladder reaches one of their kinks, and they auto-expand as needed when it does. The collapse/expand arrow beside a category or subcategory is a separate show all control: collapsed (arrow ›) is the ladder peek state, while expanded (arrow pointing down) shows all of its children at once. Expanding a subcategory shows all of its children; collapsing it again returns to the peek. When a top-3 kink lives inside a subcategory, that subcategory stays collapsed with only that one kink peeking out; click its arrow to expand it fully. Hover or tap a kink’s popularity figure and the tooltip shows a single synthesized popularity score — the one combined number the ordering is built from, with no per-source breakdown.

A quiet progress line keeps you oriented: “MM:SS elapsed · X of Y topics rated.” It’s an elapsed clock, not a countdown, and it runs whenever topics remain unrated before parking itself when the full pass is complete. Ticking Show prior ratings automatically pauses the timer while you review; untick it and the timer resumes. You can also use pause / resume whenever you want a breather, or Reset for a clean clock on a review pass. When list view opens from the profile edit dialog, the clock starts paused so a little tweaking does not inflate the original time.

The list-view header has one centered button that morphs with your situation. With no profile loaded, it reads Create profile from ratings and creates a new profile from the ratings you’ve made. With a profile loaded, the same button reads Submit what you’ve got: it saves and edits that same profile — it does not create a new one. Submit what you’ve got stays with you while you scroll, so partial progress is always worth keeping. When you finish the pass, the completion dialog includes Submit & continue, so you can submit the completed ratings and move straight on without hunting for another control. Reopen the form and it fills itself from the profile’s current hypermap ratings, with the elapsed time restored. List-view edits flow onto the visual hypermap, and hypermap edits are waiting when you return to the list. The stored profile completion time counts only the minutes when the timer is actually running; paused browsing time does not sneak into the total.

At the bottom, Submit anonymous survey unlocks only after all ~400 topics have ratings. It sends the encoded ratings, list-view timer seconds, and app version to the project’s Google Form—no name, pictures, email, or account. Submission is anonymous, limited to once per browser, and cannot be taken back. Each complete pass helps build Sexcapades’ own “how common is this kink?” dataset.

Move around the hypermap

Move around however feels natural. Use the hypermap viewport’s + and − buttons to zoom, or press the home-shaped Fit map button to bring the whole hypermap home again. On desktop, you can also scroll the mouse wheel to zoom and drag to pan; on a touch device, pinch to zoom and drag to pan. Neither the viewport controls nor the hint appears in exported images.

As you rate, the hypermap starts telling its story in color. Every rated line takes on its slider color: green is Want to do, yellow is Curious about, and red is against. These labels separate two different answers: Curious about means interested in exploring, learning, or fantasy; Want to do means consent to actual participation. Neither label turns another person’s rating into consent for you—talk together before acting. Before a first-generation branch has ratings, its line borrows the predominant hanky-code color from its children; children without a hanky code do not vote. A dashed line in that predominant color means the children are mixed with no clear majority, while dark-charcoal dashes mean none of them has a hanky code. Once a line is rated, its heat color takes over.

Prominent dotted lines reach across the hypermap to join related kinks on different branches. They’re there even on a fresh hypermap with no ratings, so you can see how the ideas connect before touching a slider. At-a-glance rings on a combined tab

On a cross-product tab such as Sam ⊗ Riley, the rings stop describing one person’s mix. They divide the comparison results produced at the chosen Cast strictness:

Center and Horizon rings

The center node carries the three green, yellow, and red at-a-glance rings. The Horizon ring sits around the header avatar and measures breadth × depth from complete profile data, not only what is visible on screen.

On a single-profile tab, breadth reflects how much of the map has been rated, category by category; depth reflects how strongly those rated answers lean toward interest rather than against. Horizon combines the two, so exploring more of the map and recording more positive interest both extend the arc.

On a cross-product tab, Horizon is calculated from the full source profiles behind the combined tab and aggregates their breadth and depth. It is not the group’s match percentage, the number of compatible topics, or the visible result after filters. A long Horizon arc can mean the participants mapped themselves thoroughly without meaning they match closely; use the center rings and match percentage to read the comparison itself.

Select profiles with Cast

Cast unlocks when two or more profiles have ratings. Before that, Sexcapades keeps the control out of the way and prompts Select two profiles to compare.

Use a wide cast while brainstorming possibilities together, including softer maybes, then move toward a narrow cast before a first scene to focus on stronger matches. Blind Comparison

Use Blind Comparison when people want to find their overlap without revealing their full hypermaps. The order matters:

  1. Set up the group comparison first. Select every profile that will be part of the comparison and create the group before importing the blind profile.
  2. The person sharing their map exports the profile with the blind flag enabled. The export still contains the full profile data, plus a marker telling Sexcapades to process it as blind.
  3. With that group comparison active, import the blind-flagged profile. Sexcapades computes the mutual match across all profiles in the group.
  4. Anything in the blind profile that is not a mutual match is permanently deleted during import—not merely hidden. The comparison keeps only the overlaps, so neither person sees the other’s full ratings.

This cannot be undone. The discarded ratings are gone from the imported profile, which is what makes Blind Comparison genuinely blind. Keep the original export if you may need the full profile later.

If a blind-flagged profile is imported when there is no active group comparison, it loads normally with all of its data. Set up the group first if you want the blind import to take effect.

Blind Comparison is different from the Blind Mode view filter. The view filter only hides non-matches and keeps the underlying ratings; Blind Comparison deletes non-matching data at import.

Scene Sentinel and GibberLink

Scene Sentinel carries a play guide into a live scene. Once Scene Setter has made a Play Guide, Sentinel keeps the Dom and sub in touch during play: the sub’s watch (GibberWear) sends check-ins to the phone, and the Dom can ask for a check-in or send the scene setup. Which watch belongs to which profile

Each profile has a short name for its watch — usually just the profile’s own key, like adam or eve. You tell a watch which profile it belongs to once, when you set it up, and from then on the Sentinel knows which watch to talk to.

Setting up a watch

In the Sentinel, pick a profile and send its setup to the watch. That tells the watch who it belongs to. You can also choose how often the watch reminds its wearer to check in — or turn reminders off, so the watch only checks in when someone asks it to.

Checking in during a scene

During a scene, the wearer taps the watch to check in, and the app shows how they’re doing — enjoyment, heat, and how much they can take. The Dom can also ask the watch to check in from the app. When a watch is set up, it answers back to confirm it got its key; if the app doesn’t hear back, it says so.

Asking every watch at once

The app can ask every watch in the room to check in at the same time, instead of one by one. That reaches every watch that’s listening, whether or not it has been set up yet.

Heat follows the profile

Each profile has a heat setting. When you sync from a profile’s Heat control, that heat copies onto its watch, so the watch shows the same heat as the profile.

Bluetooth

If the sound signals can’t be used, the phone can talk to the watch over Bluetooth instead. It pairs one-to-one, so it already knows which watch it is.

Watches without a profile yet

Signals from a watch that hasn’t been given a profile yet show up on a card of their own, so they’re never missed — a tap, a check-in, or a setup confirmation all appear there.

The simulation options were removed from the Sentinel. There are no “simulate” buttons — the Sentinel only sends and receives real signals, because real test devices (phone and watch) are available.

Share and export

For the quickest handoff: every profile tab has Copy share link. The profile’s ratings travel inside the URL itself—no account or server—and opening it creates a new profile named Unknown; the recipient can edit that name or accept it. Share deliberately: once the link leaves your hands, it cannot be taken back. The nearby “Compare hypermaps?” writer creates this paste-ready opener: Compare hypermaps? I mapped what I want to give, receive, and explore. Curious where ours overlaps? Tap Copy message and send it.

For dropping the hypermap into a chat or opening it interactively: tap the camera icon on the profile tab. The Export dialog offers a sync hypermap in SVG or compressed SVGZ, alongside PNG, low-res PNG, QR Sheet, and PDF. Choose Black, White, or Transparent for the map background, pick the format you need, inspect any preview the app opens, then download the result and send the original file.

Choose the right export

Sync hypermap — recommended: a sync hypermap is an interactive SVG export of the map currently visible on screen. It scales cleanly, keeps every detail crisp, and preserves hover tooltips—mouse over any node to see the topic name, About text, and ratings. Choose Black, White, or Transparent for the background. SVGZ packages the same sync hypermap in a smaller, gzip-compressed file.

For the full hypermap experience: embedded images—including the examples in this guide—do not expose the file’s hover tooltips, and browsers’ built-in image viewers limit how far you can zoom. Open the SVG file directly in a browser to use its hover tooltips. For hover tooltips and free zoom together, a dedicated SVG viewer is a helpful companion when exploring a dense sync hypermap in detail.

PNG visible map — share or archive: a 3600-pixel, pixel-perfect snapshot of what is currently shown on the map, available with the same Black, White, or Transparent backgrounds. The map image is clean and no longer includes an app-link QR overlay. Its visible-only profile data is embedded invisibly inside the file, so your active filters control what leaves the app.

Want a complete backup? Before exporting, turn off the Net visibility filter and set the interest slider to show everything. Then export — you’ll get your full profile. For sharing with a partner, leave the filters on to export only what you want them to see.

Sync card — print it or send its QR images: a business-card-sized handout with QR codes on the back. Large profiles split across numbered codes that scan in any order; the app stitches them back together. Every code is backed for reliable scanning, and the profile data is also embedded invisibly in the file itself, giving you two independent recovery paths.

QR Sheet — share it in a gallery or picture set: a page of clean, black-on-white QR images, with one code per image. Save the images as JPEG or PNG files, then share them by text, email, gallery, picture set, or another file-sharing method.

To import from a QR Sheet: Open sexcapades.io → tap “Scan or Import” → upload the QR Sheet image or scan it with your camera. On mobile: the button goes straight to the camera scanner. On desktop: it opens the full import dialog with all options (file upload, paste, camera if available).

Your PNG is also your save copy. Keeping the exported file preserves whatever was visible when you exported it: that data is embedded inside the image rather than printed visibly on the map. Profiles otherwise live only in the browser’s memory, so make a complete-backup PNG before you close or refresh if you want to keep the full profile for later.

Other options: the SVG sync hypermap is recommended for scalable, interactive exploration; SVGZ provides the same sync hypermap in a smaller file; low-res PNG makes a smaller copy for chats, uploads, and virtual backgrounds; QR Sheet export saves scannable profile data without the printed card; and PDF is ready for ordinary viewing or printing.

To import someone’s profile, use Scan or Import in the app. On mobile, the button opens the camera scanner directly. On desktop, it opens the full import dialog, where you can upload a JPEG or PNG QR Sheet image, paste profile data, upload an exported PNG, PDF, or sync-card file, or use a camera when one is available.

JPEG files don’t carry embedded profile data — when you upload a JPEG QR Sheet image, the app scans its visible QR code to recover your profile. PNG and PDF exports have your profile data embedded directly in the file metadata, so the app reads it from the original file itself. Map PNGs no longer include an app-link QR overlay; sync cards and QR Sheet exports keep their profile-data QR codes unchanged.

QR codes on sync cards and QR Sheets use level-H error correction, larger black-on-white rendering, and wider quiet zones, making them tolerant of ordinary JPEG recompression—including after a chat app recompresses the image.

Before creating a sync hypermap or PNG, choose the background that suits where it’s going: Black, White, or Transparent. Image exports pause in an Image preview dialog first, giving you a chance to use the zoom controls and inspect the result before clicking or tapping the separate Download button.

Low-resolution export guidance

When file size matters, Export low-res PNG downscales each embedded profile photo to 400 pixels on its long edge, helping keep the PNG under roughly 2 MB. It’s especially handy for Zoom virtual backgrounds. The original photos in the live profile stay full-resolution and untouched. The export can still carry more than one photo; if the file remains too large, remove photos before exporting to trim it further.

Every exported file is also an import file. Anyone holding an exported file may be able to read the profile data it carries, so choose who receives it with care.

Sharing Your Profile Safely

Your exported SVG, SVGZ, PNG, PDF, or sync card contains your preference data. How you share it matters — some methods will destroy that data, and some will expose it to people you didn’t intend.

Safe methods (preserve your data):

  • Direct file transfer — AirDrop, USB drive, or email attachment. The file arrives exactly as exported.
  • Signal (as a file, not a photo) — Send it as a document attachment, not through the camera/photo picker.
  • Google Drive — Works fine, but set the link to “Restricted” (specific people only), not “Anyone with the link.”

Unsafe methods (will destroy your profile data):

  • WhatsApp, Facebook Messenger, iMessage photos, Instagram — These compress and re-encode images, which strips the embedded profile data.
  • Screenshots — A screenshot is a new image, not your export. It won’t contain any profile data.

Before you share: Check what’s in your export. Sync hypermaps (SVG or SVGZ) and PNG files contain your ratings, markers, and any profile info you chose to include. If you used the “shared profile” export option, personal details are already stripped. Think about who you’re sending it to — this is intimate data.
